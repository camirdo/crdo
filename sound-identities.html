SOUND IDENTITIES

This section will contain an ongoing collection of sound recordings and short texts exploring the relationship between sound, place, memory and social experience.

Every city, neighbourhood and block possesses a distinctive sound identity. In this post-modern society, all such spaces are mediated by surveillance. The power of technology thus becomes manifest in amplifying control and vigilance, and sound itself operates as a psychological weapon capable of exerting social control and acoustic violence.

In the city, the soundscape is saturated with noises and acoustic signals that generate points of inflection, attention, and movement: alarms, buzzers, traffic jams, loudspeakers, sirens, cellphone speakers and other sounds constitute what García López (2005) describe as sonotopías of commotion. In addition, the struggle to gain power in public spaces is also carried out through sound: “Whoever shouts dominates a space, whether large or small, with the force of their utterance, and by shouting, they not only carry their voice further, but also reach more listeners through the effect of amplification. The shout is an open signal that does not discriminate between recipients; what is shouted is for everyone, and by everyone I mean those who are nearby and can hear.” (Domínguez Ruiz, 2022)

Murray Schafer classifies sonic elements as soundmarks and signals. Signals are punctual sounds consciously recognized and oriented toward the community—acoustic warnings such as bells, whistles, horns, and sirens. Soundmarks, by contrast, are “community sounds that are unique or possess specific qualities that make them particularly meaningful or noticeable to people in that place,” contributing to the “unique acoustic life of the community” (Schafer, 2001). These sounds participate in forming a sound identity: “There exists an organized construction in the individual experience of place through sound experience, giving rise to a richness and variety of sonic situations and particular soundscapes that are identified and analyzed” (Carles & Palmese, n.d.). “Sound identity refers to a distinctive sound through which individuals and groups recognize themselves and differentiate from others” (Domínguez Ruiz, 2015).
